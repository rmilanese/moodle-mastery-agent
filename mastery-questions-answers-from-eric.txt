# Lesson 1

The unit’s problem is not insufficient repetition; it is an imbalance between training and education. Completing the schedule and executing familiar drills demonstrate proficiency against known conditions. MCDP 7 explains that training prepares Marines for known factors of war, while education develops judgment for unknown and changing circumstances. Both are required for readiness. Therefore, I would retain enough repetition to sustain essential skills, but shift the unit toward learner-centered, problem-based learning. Exercises should introduce changing conditions, incomplete information, unfamiliar problems, and opportunities to develop and compare alternative solutions. After each event, candid feedback and reflection should identify the unit’s existing strengths, or “surfaces,” and its knowledge or performance “gaps.” Subsequent events should target those gaps and assess whether Marines can transfer what they learned to a new situation, rather than merely reproduce a rehearsed response.

The individual Marine must take responsibility for continuous self-development. Each Marine should understand why the task matters, recognize personal strengths and weaknesses, ask questions, seek and provide constructive feedback, reflect on decisions, and pursue learning beyond scheduled events. The Marine’s contribution is active engagement and the disciplined application of lessons to unfamiliar problems, not passive attendance.

The instructor should serve as a teacher, facilitator, and mentor. The instructor must know the learners’ strengths, weaknesses, needs, and experience; tailor methods accordingly; and use questions, scenarios, and guided problem-solving to develop critical and creative thinking. The instructor should provide timely, specific feedback, permit reasonable mistakes to become learning opportunities, and increase complexity as competence grows.

The unit leader must establish the conditions for learning. That includes prioritizing time and resources, setting a personal example of continuous learning, creating an environment where Marines can question assumptions and discuss errors candidly, and ensuring exercises realistically reflect operational uncertainty. The leader should judge success by improved competence, initiative, decision-making, and adaptability, not by completion statistics alone.

Thus, the schedule remains a supporting tool, but repeating it more often is not the remedy. The desired outcome is a unit that can execute fundamental skills instinctively while thinking, adapting, and making sound decisions when events depart from the plan.

# Lesson 2:

I would reject both parts of the staff member’s claim. Winning an engagement demonstrates tactical success, but it does not by itself prove progress toward the national goal. The tactical level concerns engagements and battles conducted to achieve assigned combat objectives. The operational level links and sequences those tactical results through major operations and campaigns. The strategic level focuses military action on achieving national policy objectives.

I would trace each victory through a causal chain: Did the engagement contribute to winning the battle? Did the battle create the conditions required by the campaign? Did the campaign advance the military strategic objective? Finally, did that military result support the desired political end state? A force could win every engagement while pursuing an ineffective campaign, consuming resources disproportionately, strengthening the adversary’s will, or producing political effects that undermine the national objective. As the reading cautions, “battlefield brilliance seldom rescues a bad strategy.”

The relationship among the levels runs in both directions. Strategy establishes aims, allocates resources, and imposes restraints and constraints. The operational level translates those aims into campaigns and creates favorable conditions for tactical action. Tactical units then produce results that operational commanders combine and exploit.

Campaign results must also inform higher leadership. If operations reveal that the strategic aim is unattainable, too costly, inadequately resourced, or inconsistent with changing political conditions, higher leaders may need to reconsider the aim, the resources committed, the accepted risk, the restraints and constraints on military action, or even whether the existing military approach remains suitable. Operational commanders must communicate those results and risks upward rather than continue pursuing an unclear or infeasible objective. Thus, strategy shapes campaigns, but strategy must also adapt to operational circumstances.

I would also reject the claim that small-unit decisions cannot affect the wider outcome. Levels of war are determined by an action’s effect or contribution, not automatically by unit size or command echelon. A small-unit action can produce operational or strategic consequences by affecting civilian support, legitimacy, coalition cohesion, public opinion, adversary propaganda, or political freedom of action. Conversely, disciplined tactical decisions can preserve legitimacy and create campaign opportunities. Campaign assessment must therefore examine whether tactical actions, operational design, and strategic purpose remain aligned. Harmony among the levels reinforces success; disharmony can turn tactical victories into strategic failure.
