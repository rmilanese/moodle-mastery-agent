# Lesson 1

The unit’s problem is not insufficient repetition; it is an imbalance between training and education. Completing the schedule and executing familiar drills demonstrate proficiency against known conditions. MCDP 7 explains that training prepares Marines for known factors of war, while education develops judgment for unknown and changing circumstances. Both are required for readiness. Therefore, I would retain enough repetition to sustain essential skills, but shift the unit toward learner-centered, problem-based learning. Exercises should introduce changing conditions, incomplete information, unfamiliar problems, and opportunities to develop and compare alternative solutions. After each event, candid feedback and reflection should identify the unit’s existing strengths, or “surfaces,” and its knowledge or performance “gaps.” Subsequent events should target those gaps and assess whether Marines can transfer what they learned to a new situation, rather than merely reproduce a rehearsed response.

The individual Marine must take responsibility for continuous self-development. Each Marine should understand why the task matters, recognize personal strengths and weaknesses, ask questions, seek and provide constructive feedback, reflect on decisions, and pursue learning beyond scheduled events. The Marine’s contribution is active engagement and the disciplined application of lessons to unfamiliar problems, not passive attendance.

The instructor should serve as a teacher, facilitator, and mentor. The instructor must know the learners’ strengths, weaknesses, needs, and experience; tailor methods accordingly; and use questions, scenarios, and guided problem-solving to develop critical and creative thinking. The instructor should provide timely, specific feedback, permit reasonable mistakes to become learning opportunities, and increase complexity as competence grows.

The unit leader must establish the conditions for learning. That includes prioritizing time and resources, setting a personal example of continuous learning, creating an environment where Marines can question assumptions and discuss errors candidly, and ensuring exercises realistically reflect operational uncertainty. The leader should judge success by improved competence, initiative, decision-making, and adaptability, not by completion statistics alone.

Thus, the schedule remains a supporting tool, but repeating it more often is not the remedy. The desired outcome is a unit that can execute fundamental skills instinctively while thinking, adapting, and making sound decisions when events depart from the plan.
